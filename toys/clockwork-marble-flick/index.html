Clockwork Marble Flick

A wind-up tabletop version of Marble Flick. One mainspring turns three nested rings at the game's own speed ratios, a fan fly keeps the pace steady, and the four hotspots trip spring-driven automata like the strike train of a clock.

NotesHow the mechanism plays the game

Ring drive. Each ring carries a crown wheel on its underside. One steel arbor runs radially beneath them along the diagonal, with three pinions of 15, 16 and 10 leaves meshing the crowns of 100, 220 and 350 teeth. All three sit on top of the arbor, so every ring turns the same way, at 0.315, 0.153 and 0.060 rad/s: the game's 0.32 : 0.15 : 0.06 within 2%.

Power and pace. A mainspring barrel of 100 teeth drives the ring arbor up through 10 and 2.5 to one, so eight turns of the spring give about ten minutes of play. The same arbor drives up 60 to one into a two-vane fan fly at about 1,200 rpm, as in a music box. Drag on the vanes rises with the square of their speed, so the fly takes up most of the change in the spring's pull, though not all of it: a stopwork lets the spring use only the middle eight of its ten turns, where its pull is steadiest, and the rings still slow a little towards the end of a wind. The winding arbor stays still while the barrel turns, held by a click, and comes out through the east wall. The ring arbor stops at 224 from the centre in a cock, an L-shaped potence screwed to the inside of the outer plate, so arbor 2's 60T wheel can turn just beyond its end. The movement is self-contained: two plates joined by four pillars, standing on the floor. Lantern pinions have pins 2mm longer than the teeth they meet, so their flanges stand clear either side of the wheel. Sheet 6 places every arbor and wheel.

Rings and seams. The rings float 1mm apart with their tops flush with the deck, and the seam edges get a 0.3mm chamfer so marbles cross without a click. Solid maple moves with the seasons by more than that 1mm, so the deck and rings are birch ply with maple veneer on both faces. Each ring runs on two caged races of ⌀6 balls, on hardened race wires let into its underside: a V-grooved race under its inner edge keeps it centred, and a flat race under its outer edge only carries it, so a ring a hair out of round can't bind. The races stand on threaded posts from the bed plate, which is cut back square under the movement. Setting the posts so the rings run level to about 0.1mm is the hardest part to make well.

Hotspot gates. Each hotspot is a ⌀34 aperture with a sprung flap 17mm below the surface. A marble crossing the middle of it faster than about 0.7 m/s bridges the gap before it can drop in; slower ones fall onto the flap. This is the game's speed rule done by gravity. A marble crossing near the edge of the aperture meets a shorter gap and gets away at a lower speed, so try the aperture on a test piece before cutting the deck. The weight of one ⌀25 marble, about 22g, is all the flap has to work with, so its Bowden cable pulls a light trip rather than a lever. The trip fires once as the flap goes down. The marble stays in the pocket, out of play, until a player lifts it out, and only then can that gate fire again.

Event release. All four cables go to the same trip, which lifts the warning lever of a separate small spring train with its own fly, like a clock's strike. The released train turns a four-cam drum a quarter turn, and a locking plate with four notches stops it there. Each cam has its lobe in a different quarter, so each release fires the next event in a fixed order: earthquake, spin surge, cat, paw. The train can't tell which gate fired, so this fixed order stands in for the game's shuffle, where each hotspot has its own event.

The four events. Earthquake: a cam lifts a weighted hammer that raps the deck underside eight times. Spin surge: a cam slides a collar along the fly arbor that feathers the vanes nearly edge-on, cutting their drag about fourteen-fold, so the rings run faster for the rest of the quarter turn. That is up to 3.7 times faster, and less in practice, since the friction of the rings doesn't drop with the vanes. Cat: an arm 60mm above the deck, high enough to clear a giant, swings an original brass cat across the board on a 240mm radius while a cam flicks its paw. A crank through the back wall drives it through a slipping clutch, so a marble in the cat's way can stop it without harm. Paw: a curved brass arm ending in a paw lies flush in a groove in the fixed collar round the hole. Its pin lifts it 12mm, a lever swings it out across the 5 ring and back, and it drops into its groove, raking whatever it catches over the collar and into the hole. Nothing stands in the bore, so sunk marbles always fall clear.

Giants. Real ⌀45 shooter marbles play the part of the game's giants: heavy, and too wide for the ⌀38 hole. They don't shrink back, so the rule becomes "one giant per player per game".

What stays manual. Scoring, turn order and the launch-pad penalty are counted by the players. Sunk marbles fall straight down the tube into a long drawer that runs back under the hole and pulls out at the front; its felt-lined floor falls 5° to the front, so each marble rolls clear of the tube's end.
